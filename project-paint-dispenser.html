Engineering Design II (Tufts) — Spring 2026 — Team Project

Assistive Paint Dispenser

An assistive device designed with and for an individual with cerebral palsy, helping her dispense her own paint colors independently.

Tools SolidWorks Waterjet Milling 3D Printing Vacuum Forming Manual Bending
Deliverables Low-Fidelity Prototype Final Prototype Demonstration Video Design Poster

Project Overview

Our client, an individual with cerebral palsy, loves painting but has always needed help from her art teacher or mother to open paint tubes and squeeze out the right amount of color. We wanted to give her the ability to select and dispense her own paints independently, so she could paint on her own terms. Working within a $60 budget, our team followed a full user-centered design process, from initial observation through a final device delivered directly to her.

Engineering Approach

We started with task analysis and market research, looking at existing assistive and industrial dispensing products across other fields before defining our own user needs and engineering requirements. Early in ideation, we joked about using a hydraulic press, clearly overkill for a paint tube, but the joke pointed at something real: more mechanical advantage would make it much easier to squeeze paint out with minimal hand force. That became one of our three initial concepts, and the one we favored from early on, though we still developed and pitched all three to our client before committing to it. We built out full pitches for each — sketches, drawings, and a short pitch video — and after her feedback, moved forward with the lever-and-press design, developing custom dies shaped specifically to squeeze paint out of the tube efficiently.

Design Iteration

Our low-fidelity prototype was laser-cut acrylic, taped together, to test the overall sizing and fit with the paint tubes before committing to final materials.

For the final version, we moved to aluminum for durability, stability, and portability. Parts were cut on a waterjet, with additional milling and drilling for assembly.

One early open question was how to hinge the mechanism at all — a standard hinge wouldn't work because the paint tube itself has thickness, so there needed to be a gap between the two plates even when closed. We solved this by mounting the hinge on a raised block, creating consistent spacing regardless of position. L-brackets were bent by hand on a manual bender to complete the frame.

We also 3D-printed custom dies to keep the tube properly seated. I designed these myself, including a countersunk feature to help guide and center the tube, and had to quickly shorten the top die after our first version turned out too long.

To make the paint trays easy to read at a glance, we made them from clear plastic, testing different thicknesses to balance rigidity against flexibility. As a final touch, we vacuum-formed a Disney-themed imprint into the trays, since our client is a Disney fan.

Key Challenges

Not everything got fully resolved. The hinge had very little resistance on its own, so it could slam shut unexpectedly, and opening it fully to reduce that risk pushed the handle out of comfortable reach from a seated position — a real tradeoff we didn't have time to fully solve. Adding some adjustable resistance so the hinge could hold at any position would be a clear next step.

We also tried off-the-shelf silicone toothpaste caps to keep the tube opening from drying out between uses, but they didn't seal well enough. Instead, we relied on the lever's mechanical advantage to push out any dried paint at the tip, and noted in the instructions that a caregiver should help open and close the cap at the start and end of each session.

Final Outcome

We delivered the final device along with written instructions and a video demonstration. Our client attended the final poster presentation over Zoom and seemed happy with the result, though we don't have confirmation of how it performed for her in ongoing use.
