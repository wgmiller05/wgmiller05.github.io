Custom Instron Grips for Round-Sample Testing

Custom grips designed and machined to enable round-sample mechanical testing on the university's Instron, extending an existing test setup built only for flat dog-bone samples.

Tools SolidWorks Lathe Threading
Deliverables CAD Assembly Engineering Drawing Physical Grips

Project Overview

The university's Instron test setup only had grips built for flat dog-bone samples, but an upcoming testing program required grips that could hold round samples instead. Before that testing could begin, we needed to design, build, and validate new grip hardware — practicing on surrogate materials (a carbon fiber tube, aluminum rod, and corrugated metal tube) rather than the actual test samples.

Engineering Approach

The PhD student had designed the V-bend geometry that would contact and grip the round samples, and tasked me with figuring out how to make the rest of the system work — designing the grip body, supporting the grippers, and making it compatible with the university's Instron, which only had grips built for flat dog-bone samples. I designed a C-frame body, open on one side so a sample can pass through, which meant the grippers couldn't be supported from underneath. I added two steel rods spanning through both sides of the frame and through both grippers to support them structurally. For the two attachments connecting the grip body to the Instron's pin fixture, I found an existing steel piece in the shop's scrap stock that just needed re-threading, and machined the second attachment from scratch out of aluminum.

Design Iteration

The main grip body, which holds and positions the parts that actually contact the sample, was machined by the PhD student. My work focused on the two attachments that connect that body to the Instron's pin fixture, one per side. One started as an existing steel piece I found in the machine shop's stock. I turned down its original threads and re-cut new ones to interface with my grip body. The other I machined entirely from scratch out of aluminum. I also cut the threads joining each attachment to the body, threaded the interface for the lead screw (the lead screw itself was an off-the-shelf part), and machined the steel support rods.

The rods started as steel rod stock, but machining them on a standard lathe setup meant turning one side, then flipping the stock to do the other. This introduced small diameter inaccuracies where the two sides met. To fix this, I turned them between centers instead, machining the entire rod in a single continuous setup for a consistent diameter along its full length.

The first iteration used aluminum V-bend pieces (reinforced with a steel backing plate) as the sample-contact surface.

Key Challenges

The aluminum V-bend contact surfaces didn't grip the round samples firmly enough, causing slipping during testing. This is problematic, since the whole point of this phase was validating the video extensometer and temperature chamber setup, which required the sample to stay securely in place. To fix this, the PhD student redesigned the sample-contact pieces as fully round, solid-steel components, better matched to the round sample geometry. This final version gripped securely with no slipping. These grips served their purpose as a first-pass validation step for the lab's testing setup.
